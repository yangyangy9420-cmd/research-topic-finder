# Methodology: ethical “paper imitation” for topic discovery

## Core interpretation

Use the user's source methodology as an efficiency framework, but reinterpret it ethically:

- do not start from empty imagination; start from excellent prior work.
- imitate research patterns, not sentences, data, or conclusions.
- choose topics that reuse the lab's strengths before chasing new directions.
- treat literature reading as a tool for solving research problems, not as a performance ritual.

## Topic selection principles

1. Prefer the lab's advantage direction.
   - existing instruments, datasets, code, samples, test rigs, protocols, advisor expertise, senior students' work, funded projects, and known publication routes.
   - advantages reduce uncertainty and make the topic easier to execute.

2. Avoid isolated new directions.
   - a new direction with no platform, no inherited data, no equipment, no advisor familiarity, and no senior-student experience is high risk.
   - only accept a new direction if it can be connected to an old advantage direction.

3. Map the “lab inventory.”
   - research directions and funded projects.
   - past theses and papers from the group.
   - equipment and software.
   - sample/data access.
   - advisor's preferred methods and evaluation standards.
   - publication history and typical journals.

4. Choose the fastest credible path.
   - a good graduate-student topic is not necessarily the most revolutionary topic.
   - prefer topics with clear data collection, reproducible methods, obvious baselines, and visible paper structure.

## WWH to Ideas reading method

For each paper or paper cluster:

- Why: what problem, pain point, mechanism, or engineering demand motivates the work.
- What: what result, conclusion, prediction, model, dataset, or claim the paper contributes.
- How: what data, equipment, method, experiment, software, model, validation, and metrics make it work.
- Ideas: what limitation, assumption, missing scenario, weak validation, or untested variable can become a new topic.

## Innovation patterns

### Application innovation

Use an existing method/theory on a new object, scenario, region, dataset, device, or engineering workflow. Verify that the transferred method remains valid.

Typical forms:

- mature computer-vision method applied to a less-studied crop/material/disease.
- a validated sensor or model moved from lab conditions to field conditions.
- a method from another discipline adapted to the user's domain.

### Modification innovation

Modify an existing method to handle a limitation.

Typical forms:

- add a correction coefficient, attention module, feature branch, calibration step, environmental factor, or robustness test.
- simplify a complex model for low-cost deployment.
- replace a component with a lighter or more interpretable one.

### Fusion innovation

Combine multiple valid methods or data sources into a pipeline.

Typical forms:

- classical feature extraction plus deep learning.
- visual data plus time-series/environmental variables.
- detection plus tracking plus prediction.
- experimental data plus simulation.

### Comparison innovation

Benchmark methods and derive selection rules or an improved combined method.

Typical forms:

- compare several models across scenarios, devices, lighting, sample types, or metrics.
- identify where each method fails and propose practical guidelines.

### Method/theory innovation

Develop a new method or theory. Recommend this only when the user has strong data, strong technical capacity, and enough validation routes.

## Practical output logic

A useful topic recommendation should answer:

1. What should I study?
2. Why is it worth studying now?
3. What strong papers can I imitate structurally?
4. What can I do with my instruments and advisor's focus?
5. Where is the smallest publishable innovation?
6. What should I not do because the risk is too high?
